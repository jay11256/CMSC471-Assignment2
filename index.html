NYPD Allegations Case Study

Contextualizing Outcomes: Substantiated vs. Dismissed Complaints

White Hat Visual

Outcome Differentiation (1985–2020)

White Hat Write Up

This visualization uses the New York City Civilian Complaint Review Board (CCRB) dataset, which documents civilian complaints filed against NYPD officers from the past 4 decades. The dataset categorizes allegations into 4 types: force, abuse of authority, discourtesy, and offensive language. These types are further broken down into subtypes, which we decided to not include to not overwhelm viewers. The dataset also records how each complain was resolved, which we included into the visual.

The raw data was transformed into the current visual through aggregation and selective exclusion. For each year, we summed the total amount of each complaint and their subtypes in order to create an accurate and representative plot. The original dataset ranges from 1985-2020, but we noticed that the data for 2020 was abnormally sparse (all occurrences summed to less than 10). Even accounting for COVID-19 (which likely caused the drop in 2019), the values for 2020 were far too low. We concluded that the current version of the dataset available to download was cutoff in the early 2020s, leading to unrepresentative values for that year, so we decided to excluded 2020 from the domain.

Each aesthetic aspect of the visual was designed to easily portray the types of complaints the CCRB has received over the years. Each allegation category gets its own color, making it easy to distinguish them for each other. Within these categories, we plotted the substatianted complaints and the dismissed complaints separately to ensure we highlight the “true” complaints. This was encoded by contrasting solid and dashed lines, as both dashed lines and dismissed complaints can be associated with “unrealness” while substantiated complaints can be associated with solidity.

Despite have 8 lines plotted on one graph, we maintain clarity by allowing the user to highlight different lines and by using tooltips. When hovering over any line in the legend, the corresponding line in the graph will retain its color while the others get grayed out, essentially “highlighting” the line the user is interested in. This allows users to view each line plot one at a time. When users hover over the plot itself, a tooltip for that year shows up. It displays the specific numerical values for all 8 lines, allowing users to make accurate comparisons between them.

Black Hat Visual

Ethnic Distribution of Allegations

Black Hat Write Up

The black hat visualization focuses on distorting perceptions of how race and number of complaints are correlated. Part of how the visual accomplishes this is through omitting important contextual information. Firstly, it categorizes number of complaints by race, but only graphs the three races with the most complaints (White, Hispanic, Black), ignoring Asian, American Indian, and Other. This directs the reader to only consider these three races, which do not fully represent the NYPD. The visual also displays the total count of allegations per race, neglecting to mention several important considerations like: - Board Disposition: most allegations are either unsubstantiated (CCRB investigated but could not affirmatively conclude that the conduct occurred and broke the rules) or exonerated (CCRB investigated and concluded that conduct did not violate the NYPD's rules). However, the visual groups them all, suggesting a higher frequency of misconduct than is proven. - Party: the visual does not explicitly state whether the race is that of the complainant or the member of service, potentially leading to different (incorrect) interpretations. - MOS Demographics: the number of complaints are not normalized/adjusted with regard to the number of MOS who fall into each category. For example, White people make up the largest portion of MOS, contributing to the larger proportion of complaints brought against them. This is not accounted for in the visual, making it difficult to draw truthful conclusions about how complaints are distributed for each race. - Time: the visual lacks contextualizing information about the time period covered or how the frequency of complaints changed over time. More maliciously, the visual highlights the number of complaints brought against White MOS by truncating the lower portion of the graph. This distorts the visual proportions of each bar, suggesting that White MOS receive a much higher proportion of allegations. This is further emphasized by the color scale.
